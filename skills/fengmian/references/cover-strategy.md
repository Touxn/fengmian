# Cover strategy

Read for new concepts, cover wording, or reference analysis. Start from the content and audience before selecting a visual style.

## Define one credible promise

Identify the viewer's task, desired result, problem, or decision. A search-oriented viewer may need a recognizable tool or task; a discovery viewer may need a striking result or an interesting unresolved question. Use the actual placement and brief rather than treating either pattern as universal.

State a single primary reason to watch in plain language. Ground it in what the video actually delivers. Emotional appeal can support that promise but should not exaggerate the evidence. If the content supports several benefits, choose a lead benefit and let the publishing title or video carry the others.

Distinguish an observable result from a claim about how it was achieved. A polished slide can demonstrate appearance; it cannot by itself prove the time spent, cost, reliability, or ease of the process.

## Choose evidence before decoration

Inspect user-provided finished work, relevant footage, screenshots, and comparisons. A strong real result is often a useful main image for AI creation tutorials. An attractive image unrelated to the actual output is not evidence.

Possible approaches include a finished artifact, a meaningful before/after, a decisive comparison, a host reaction, a visible task, or a simple metaphor. These are options, not fixed slots or a preference ranking. Choose according to the promise and available material.

For comparisons:

- Make the compared states easy to distinguish while preserving the shared subject or relevant conditions.
- Do not fabricate a poor baseline, hide decisive differences, or distort relative scale to imply an unsupported result.
- Label states only when needed for understanding. Preserve an approved comparison's wording.
- If the imagery is illustrative, do not present it as an actual experiment or delivered result.

An interface crop can demonstrate a feature when its meaning survives at the target size. A full dashboard with unreadable text may need a closer crop or a simplified explanation. A metaphor can explain an abstract relationship, but should not bury useful real evidence.

## Coordinate image, cover wording, and publishing title

Assign each component useful work:

| Component | Possible contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Image | Result, evidence, contrast, situation, or emotion |
| Cover wording | Sharpened benefit, question, comparison, or task label |
| Publishing title | Tool, audience, method, scope, or context |

These roles may overlap where necessary for recognition. Avoid duplication that consumes space without improving understanding. The image need not explain the entire topic without its publishing title.

Use wording that matches the content. A result, pain point, question, or concrete comparison may work better than a generic label. “全解” and “实战” remain available when accurate; do not append them by habit. Tool names and versions are useful when they matter to the audience's decision.

There is no universal character count. Prefer the shortest wording that preserves the meaningful promise and reads at the intended size. If the user fixed the text, keep every character and solve the layout. Suggest alternative wording only within delegated scope.

Do not invent rankings, “free” claims, time savings, percentages, or guarantees. Steps and version numbers identify structure or context; they do not establish quality. A visible screenshot of a claim does not verify the claim.

## Present three meaningful concepts

For a new unselected topic, follow the selection workflow in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). Make the alternatives differ in the reason to click or the relationship shown, not just color, clothing, or props. Do not force every task into a result/comparison/metaphor trio.

Keep each proposal concise:

- Primary promise and target viewer
- Main image and what it demonstrates
- Suggested cover wording and relationship to the publishing title
- Person's contribution, if any
- Main advantage and concrete limitation

Recommend one with a content-based reason. For example, a finished-result concept may make the quality visible, while a detailed workflow concept may lose clarity at feed size. Avoid arbitrary scores and pass thresholds without a defined measurement.

Once a concept is selected, execute its agreed outputs. Local edits and ratio extensions retain that concept unless the user requests a new direction.

## Learn from references without copying a costume

Separate what can be seen from why it may work:

- Observation: image area, text, grouping, contrast, displayed outcome, and visible relationship.
- Interpretation: the possible audience need, curiosity gap, or trust signal.
- Transfer: the part of that mechanism useful for the current content and evidence.

Do not infer causal performance from public views or a successful channel's branding. Topic, audience, distribution, publishing time, and video quality also matter. Evaluate a reference's fit to this user's audience and placement.

A productive adaptation keeps the useful relationship while inventing an original composition. Face substitution into another creator's distinctive scene does not accomplish that.

## Review the concept

Check whether the promise is accurate, the supporting image is relevant, and the main relationship survives small display sizes. Look for competing messages, redundant wording, and an unexplained gap between the video and its cover. Give specific reasons for revision rather than unsupported numeric ratings.

Consult [evidence and testing](evidence-and-testing.md) for the source basis and optional feedback loop. A legible design remains a hypothesis until the relevant audience response is observed.
