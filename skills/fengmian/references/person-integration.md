# Person integration

Read this reference whenever a cover contains a person. Make the person a functional part of the visual sentence rather than an isolated portrait placed beside the topic.

## Integration plan

Define these fields before choosing the person's location:

| Field | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Narrative role | Guide, operator, tester, builder, curator, analyst, witness, or learner |
| Action verb | Hold, support, open, operate, steer, compare, reveal, inspect, wear, enter, or present |
| Gaze target | Viewer, main object, result, or comparison target |
| Contact point | Hand, arm, torso, seat, tool, device, surface, or costume |
| Weight cue | Grip, pressure, compression, bend, support, balance, or reaction |
| Shared light | One key direction and one scene color cast |
| Depth relation | In front of, behind, inside, seated in, partially covered by, or holding the main object |
| Scene consequence | What changes in the scene because the person is present? |

Reject a route when the person has no clear answer for role, action, and scene consequence.

Require all three coupling types:

- Physical coupling through holding, supporting, gripping, wearing, sitting in, or overlapping
- Visual coupling through shared light, reflected color, contact shadow, perspective, and depth
- Narrative coupling through gaze, gesture, expression, and scene response aimed at the same topic

Use at least two meaningful interaction cues for tutorial covers. Strong pairs include support plus pointing, grip plus gaze, or operation plus visible feedback.

## Action cluster

Treat the person and dominant object as one narrative cluster.

- Connect the hand to the object through visible contact and plausible anatomy.
- Let gaze and body direction reinforce the same target.
- Choose one coherent gaze loop. A guide may look at the viewer while the gesture lands on the topic. An explorer may look at the discovery. An operator may look at the interface being controlled.
- Make a pointing finger land on a semantic target such as the product name, icon, control, or result. Keep the fingertip within roughly one-quarter of a face width from that target at final composition scale.
- Let the object respond to the person's action through tilt, opening, activation, motion, light, or a visible result.
- Keep the person's action readable at thumbnail size.
- Make the contact target large enough to survive at 10 percent size. Reject a tiny connector, button, or distant icon as the primary action target.
- Use one primary action. Avoid holding one object, pointing at another, and looking at a third.

Examples:

- Tutorial: open or support the manual while pointing to its key area.
- Workflow: operate the first step while the result appears along the same direction.
- Tool review: hold or use the actual device and look toward its result.
- Collection: stand inside or beside a shelf while selecting one item.
- Navigation metaphor: sit in the cockpit, hold the wheel, or point along the route.
- Upgrade: activate or reveal the upgraded module while reacting to the change.

## Physical coherence

- Show the object resting against the hand, arm, body, desk, or seat when weight is implied.
- Preserve five plausible fingers, natural wrist angle, continuous forearm, and correct shoulder connection.
- Add contact shadows where skin, clothing, and object meet.
- Break any white keyline or glow inside a physical contact zone so skin and object do not remain visually separated.
- Match object perspective to the person's hand and body angle.
- Match scale so the object can realistically be held, operated, entered, or worn.
- Let clothing fold, fingers compress, or posture shift when the person carries weight.
- Treat an object wider than roughly 1.5 head widths as too large for effortless single-palm support. Use two-hand support, visible body effort, a desk, or an explicit holographic and lightweight treatment.
- Regenerate the full person-object cluster when the interaction changes. Avoid adding isolated arms or hands onto an existing body.

## Lighting and color

- Use one key-light direction across face, clothing, hands, object, and background.
- Apply the same scene color cast and reflected light to skin, clothing, and nearby objects.
- Let bright objects cast subtle light onto the hand or face when physically close.
- Match shadow softness and contrast across person and environment.
- Keep the apparent key-light directions on the person and object within roughly 30 degrees of each other.
- Harmonize contrast, sharpness, color temperature, grain, and highlight softness when combining a photographic person with a 3D object.
- Use a topic-colored rim light only when it comes from a believable scene source.
- Keep skin tone even and faithful to the portrait reference after adding environmental color.

## Depth and overlap

- Allow the main object to overlap an arm, hand, or lower torso when this creates believable depth.
- Let a hand appear partly behind and partly in front of an object when gripping it.
- Use foreground, middle ground, and background cues around the person.
- Create at least one alternating occlusion when hands interact with an object, such as wrist behind the object and fingertips in front.
- Continue atmosphere, light rays, furniture, or environmental surfaces behind and around the body.
- Keep the face, eyes, and headline clear.
- Avoid isolating the person in an empty corner with no shared depth plane.

## Edge treatment

- Preserve a clean silhouette without automatically applying a thick full-body white outline.
- Use natural local contrast when the background already separates the person.
- Use a narrow white keyline, soft shadow, or colored rim only where separation is needed.
- Vary edge visibility with the scene. Some edges can blend softly into atmosphere while the face and active hands remain crisp.
- Avoid a uniform sticker border that visually detaches the person from the environment.
- Remove the keyline inside every grip, support, seat, and body-object contact area.

## Seasonal wardrobe and rotation

- Resolve wardrobe from the target publication season and climate first. Use the current local date and location when publication context is absent. Use a climate-neutral outfit when the season remains uncertain.
- In summer or a clear hot-weather context, use one breathable layer by default. Choose among a solid short-sleeve T-shirt, knit polo, breathable polo, or short-sleeve woven shirt. Avoid jackets, blazers, coats, hoodies, overshirts, and layered outerwear unless the user explicitly requests them.
- Inspect up to three recent approved covers when available. For a new and unrelated cover, change at least two of these attributes from the most recent cover: garment category, neckline or collar, sleeve treatment, dominant color, and fabric character.
- Keep wardrobe consistent across 3:4, 4:3, and 16:9 outputs that belong to the same title or campaign. Apply rotation when moving to a new title, topic, or campaign.
- Treat portrait, pose, layout, and earlier-cover clothing as non-binding references. Preserve identity, pose intent, and campaign language while generating a fresh season-appropriate outfit.
- Let wardrobe support the person's role and topic metaphor. Use ordinary clothing for practical tutorials and serious analysis. Use role-specific clothing only when it clarifies the subject.
- Carry product colors through restrained garment color, piping, reflected light, or one small accent. Avoid relying on the same navy jacket for brand continuity.
- Keep garments free of invented logos, decorative English, and illegible text.
- Match facial energy, posture, and hand action to the strength of the cover promise while preserving the requested natural expression.
- Preserve the user's recognizable face, hair, glasses, age, and overall personal identity.

## Composition by ratio

### 3:4

- Use the lower field as one connected person-object scene.
- Let arms extend across the center when supporting or operating the main object.
- Avoid placing a static half-body portrait beside an unrelated large object.

### 4:3

- Use the wider center field for a full interaction arc from object to hands to face.
- Let the person move inward from the right edge when the action needs contact.
- Preserve the headline zone while allowing natural body-object overlap below it.

### 16:9

- Make the person-object cluster compact and readable at small thumbnail size.
- Use one strong contact pose and reduce secondary scene details.
- Keep the face and active hands within the safe central area when platform cropping is possible.

## Shared scene anchor

- Place the main glow, light focus, surface, or perspective cue across the face-hand-object relationship zone.
- Avoid lighting the object dramatically while leaving the person on a flat unrelated background.
- In wider ratios, keep the interaction triangle compact. Do not use added width as empty separation between the hand and target.
- In wider ratios, prefer a large support, hold, open, or operate pose over a small fingertip tap.
- Let reflected color from a bright object or title appear subtly on nearby skin and clothing.

## Prompt construction

Describe the interaction in one physically explicit sentence:

`The person [action] the [object] with [contact point], looks toward [target], and the object [visible response].`

Then state:

- Which image is the identity authority
- Which image is a pose reference only
- Which season and climate control the wardrobe
- Which garment is required and which reference clothing must be ignored
- Whether the output continues an existing campaign or rotates to a new outfit
- Where weight or pressure appears
- How the hand connects to the wrist, arm, and shoulder
- Which object may overlap the body
- Shared light direction and color cast
- Invariants for face, text, and approved campaign style

## Failure modes

- Stock head-and-torso portrait placed in a blank corner
- Generic pointing hand aimed at empty space
- Tiny fingertip action that disappears at thumbnail size
- Palm positioned near an object without carrying its weight
- Floating prop with no grip, support, or contact shadow
- Hand, wrist, forearm, and shoulder that do not form one continuous pose
- Thick uniform white outline around the whole body
- Face and body lit from a different direction than the scene
- Clothing unrelated to the role or topic
- Copying a jacket, outer layer, or exact outfit from a reference without checking the season
- Reusing the same garment silhouette and color combination across consecutive unrelated covers
- Changing wardrobe between ratios of the same campaign without a user request
- Gaze, gesture, body direction, and scene response that lead to unrelated narrative targets
- Main object and person rendered at incompatible scale or perspective
- Background stopping behind the person's cutout edge
- Photographic person and 3D object with unrelated sharpness, grain, contrast, or highlight style
- Large object balanced on one relaxed palm without body effort or a lightweight visual explanation
- Pointing finger whose projected line stops in empty background

## Validation tests

1. **Removal test**: If removing the person leaves the visual story unchanged, strengthen the person's role or action.
2. **Contact test**: Confirm visible contact, pressure, weight, or operation at every touch point.
3. **Gaze test**: Confirm the eyes, hands, and body direction support one target.
4. **Light test**: Confirm one coherent key direction, color cast, and shadow softness.
5. **Depth test**: Confirm natural overlap and shared foreground, middle ground, or background cues.
6. **Silhouette test**: Confirm clean separation without a uniform sticker effect.
7. **Thumbnail test**: Confirm the action remains understandable at approximately 10 percent size.
8. **Coupling test**: Confirm at least one physical, one visual, and one narrative coupling.
9. **Interaction test**: Confirm two useful interaction points when space permits.
10. **Pointer test**: Confirm the fingertip lands on a semantic target within roughly one-quarter of a face width.
11. **Material test**: Confirm compatible sharpness, grain, contrast, color temperature, and highlight softness.
12. **Scale test**: Confirm the pose and body effort can explain the object's size and weight.
13. **Contact-edge test**: Inspect at 200 percent and confirm no air gap, double outline, or glow seam remains at contact points.
14. **Blur test**: Convert mentally or physically to grayscale and slight blur; confirm face, active hand, and object still form one closed visual triangle.
15. **Season test**: Confirm garment weight, sleeve treatment, and layering fit the publication season and climate.
16. **Rotation test**: For a new cover, confirm the outfit differs meaningfully from the most recent unrelated cover when history is available.
17. **Campaign test**: Confirm all ratios of one title use the same outfit unless the user requests coordinated variation.
