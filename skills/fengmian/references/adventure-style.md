# Optional adventure and journey style

Read this reference only after an adventure or journey route has been selected. It is not a default scene, palette, or layout for unrelated covers.

## Visual thesis

Build one visible transformation journey. Let the creator or dominant object enter the subject, pass through the work, and approach a finished result.

Use this visual sentence:

`headline + active vehicle or terrain + continuous task path + visible destination`

The grammar can fit learning, building, research, automation, creative production, and other subjects where progress through stages is the actual story.

## Scene architecture

1. Begin with the named input, tool, question, or starting state.
2. Show one or two large milestones along a single path.
3. Put the finished artifact or changed state at the destination.
4. Make body direction, gaze, motion, and light follow the same path when a person is present.

Turn the result into a vehicle, terrain, bridge, portal, stage, or environment. Avoid a collage of cards, a conventional desktop setup, or disconnected screens.

## Person and object coupling

- Make the person an explorer, rider, operator, or builder rather than a spectator.
- Use one readable contact point and a clear forward body angle.
- Show believable balance, speed, wind, grip, support, or weight.
- Let the free hand or gaze lead toward the destination.
- Continue the same perspective, sunlight, atmosphere, and reflected color across person and world.
- Keep the face natural; put most of the energy into action and environment.

If no portrait is supplied, let a vehicle, tool, artifact, or neutral silhouette carry the journey instead of inventing a recognizable person.

## Atmosphere and palette

Prefer a bright world when it fits the subject: open sky, visible horizon, warm destination light, readable terrain, and one path accent. Other subjects may use a studio, city, workshop, archive, or abstract landscape.

Technology light should clarify the path or transformation, not replace the whole environment. Make the destination visibly different from the starting state.

## Typography relationships

For a 3:4 adventure cover:

- Reserve the top 25 to 28 percent for typography.
- Place the main row near 5 to 7 percent from the top.
- Keep the main row around 72 to 78 percent of canvas width.
- Place the second row near 19 to 21 percent from the top.
- Keep the second row around 52 to 62 percent of canvas width.
- Preserve a visible gap between rows and keep both clear of the face or destination.

Use an energetic condensed display treatment only when it matches the subject. Preserve exact characters through deterministic typography assembly when possible, and use only fonts licensed for the intended distribution.

## Generation workflow

1. Protect the title zone before scene generation.
2. Generate the vehicle or terrain, path, milestones, and destination as one coherent world.
3. Inspect the scene at thumbnail size without typography.
4. Add exact title text with the available design or compositing workflow.
5. Inspect full size and thumbnail size.
6. Adjust title width within the composition before rebuilding a scene that is otherwise sound.

## Rejection gates

Reject and rebuild when:

- the journey has no clear start or endpoint
- the active subject can be removed without changing the story
- the result object reads as decoration rather than vehicle, terrain, or destination
- the scene resembles a dashboard or technology showroom
- title rows overlap, merge into one mass, or cover the destination
- the main row is not the first focal point at thumbnail size
- banners, thick sticker outlines, or large soft shadows detach the title from the world
